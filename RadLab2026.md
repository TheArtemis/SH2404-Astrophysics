One piece of advice is to start your observing project as soon as possible. The weather in Stockholm is highly unreliable, so you should take the chance to observe whenever you can. Starting early also gives you a better chance of observing changes over time.

Another piece of advice is to let your eyes adapt to the darkness for around 20 minutes. You will then see much more. Avoid using a flashlight or using a phone since it destroys your night sight. You could use a reddened flashlight.

### Part 2.2: Find out what can be observed

The goal of this exercise is to find out before an observation which objects can be expected to be visible at the time of the observation. In the following, you should assume that the observation takes place on February 14 at 18:00, and that the observing site has the position 59°21′ N, 18°0′ E (AlbaNova, Stockholm).

We will focus on determining the visibility of a few interesting objects, which are listed in the table on the next page. For each of these objects, you should determine its position in the sky and assess whether it can be seen or not. This is done in several steps to facilitate the calculation. First we need to calculate the hour angle, \(t\), of each object, which gives its position in the east-west direction. Remember that the hour angle is measured clockwise from the south.

#### 1) Compute the sidereal time, \(\Theta\) (the hour angle, \(t\), of the vernal equinox, \(\Upsilon\))

At noon on March 20, the Vernal Equinox is in the south, so its hour angle, i.e. the sidereal time, is \(\Theta = 0\text{ h }0\text{ m }0\text{ s}\). This is not entirely correct, but it is close enough for our purposes.

i) Given that the Vernal Equinox shifts toward the west by 4 m each day, compute the sidereal time at noon on February 14.  
ii) We wish to observe in the early evening. Compute the sidereal time at 18:00 on February 14.

#### 2) Compute the hour angle, \(t\), of the star

The hour angle of a star gives the time that has passed since the star crossed the meridian. At high latitudes this can be used to estimate its position in the sky in the following way: 0 h = South, 6 h = West, 12 h = North, and 18 h = East. The hour angle of a star can be found from the difference between the hour angle of the Vernal Equinox and the right ascension, \(\alpha\), of the star:

\[
t = \Theta - \alpha
\]

Now we know in which direction of the sky to look for the star, but we still do not know how high in the sky it is (its altitude, \(h\)).

#### 3) Compute the altitude, \(h\), of the star

Our next step is therefore to estimate this. Doing an exact calculation is complicated, so we will instead make an estimate of the approximate altitude based on the highest and lowest altitude, and the hour angle we computed above. We will do this in several steps. The table below gives the declination, \(\delta\), of each object, i.e. its height above the celestial equator.

i) Show, by drawing or some other method, that the maximum height of a star above the horizon is:

\[
h^* = \delta^* + 90^\circ - \text{lat}
\]

ii) Use this to find a relation between the declination of a star and its minimum height above or below the horizon.  
iii) Use these relations to compute the highest and lowest position in the sky for each object in the table below.  
iv) Since an object is highest in the sky when it is in the south, and lowest in the north, corresponding to hour angles of 0 and 12, you should now be able to make an estimate of the position of each object based on the hour angle you computed earlier.

Your task is now to determine the position in the sky for each of the objects in the table, given for February 14 at 18:00, and to discuss whether or not you would expect to be able to see the object during the night of the observation. If the object is not visible at 18:00, do you expect it to be visible at some other time of the night? Why/why not?
