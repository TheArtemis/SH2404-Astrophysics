## Logbook and observation notes

I made all observations from the park near Ulriksdal Palace, behind the gardens, at approximately 59.3900051 N, 18.006606 E. I chose this place because the surrounding trees blocked some direct light from the horizon, while the park was also away from the most brightly lit streets. The trees also meant that the lowest part of the horizon was partly hidden, so I could not judge objects very close to the horizon very well.

## Observation 1

Date: 1 May 2026

Time: 21:30

Location: Ulriksdal Palace park, 59.3900051 N, 18.006606 E

Weather: The sky was clear during my observation, and I could observe without clouds blocking the main targets.

Sky conditions: The sky was still affected by bright twilight, especially toward the west and north-west. The Moon was very bright, so the sky background was bright and faint objects were difficult to see. The horizon was partly blocked by trees.

Equipment: Naked-eye observation.

Objects:
- Big Dipper / Ursa Major: I could see the Big Dipper high in the sky toward the north-west/west. The seven main stars were easy to identify even though the sky was still bright.
- Cassiopeia: I could see Cassiopeia lower toward the north-east. The W shape was visible, but it was less clear than the Big Dipper.
- Orion: I could not see Orion as a clear constellation.
- Sirius: I could not identify Sirius.
- Pleiades: I could not see the Pleiades.
- Milky Way: I could not see the Milky Way. The twilight, local light pollution, and bright Moon made the sky too bright.
- Moon: I saw the Moon low toward the south-east/east. It was very bright and reduced the contrast of the surrounding sky.
- Venus: I could not confidently identify Venus because the western sky was still bright and partly obstructed.
- Jupiter: I saw a bright object in the western/north-western part of the sky that I identified as Jupiter. It was much easier to see than most stars once the sky became darker.
- Mars: I could not see Mars.

Notes:
The easiest naked-eye targets during this observation were the Moon, Jupiter, the Big Dipper, and Cassiopeia. Fainter objects were not visible because of the bright Moon, twilight, and city light pollution.

## Observation 2

Date: 1 May 2026

Time: 23:30

Location: Ulriksdal Palace park, 59.3900051 N, 18.006606 E

Weather: The sky was still clear enough for naked-eye observation.

Sky conditions: The sky was darker than at 21:30, but it was still not completely dark. The bright Moon remained the strongest source of natural sky brightness. Trees continued to block the lowest part of the horizon.

Equipment: Naked-eye observation.

Objects:
- Big Dipper / Ursa Major: I could still see the Big Dipper clearly and high in the sky. Compared with the earlier observation, it had shifted westward.
- Cassiopeia: I could see Cassiopeia toward the north-east/north. It was still lower than the Big Dipper and had also shifted slightly.
- Orion: I could not see Orion.
- Sirius: I could not identify Sirius.
- Pleiades: I could not see the Pleiades.
- Milky Way: I could not see the Milky Way because of moonlight and local light pollution.
- Moon: I saw the Moon higher than before. It had moved from low in the south-east/east toward the southern sky and was still very bright.
- Venus: I could not confidently identify Venus. The western horizon was low, bright, and partly obstructed.
- Jupiter: I could still see Jupiter, but it was lower toward the west/north-west than during the first observation.
- Mars: I could not see Mars.

Notes:
The main change over the two hours was the apparent westward motion of the sky caused by Earth's rotation. The Moon was higher, while Jupiter was lower. The Big Dipper remained the easiest constellation marker, and Cassiopeia was still visible but less prominent.

## Observation 3

Date: 10 May 2026

Time: 21:30

Location: Ulriksdal Palace park, 59.3900051 N, 18.006606 E

Weather: The early evening sky was usable for observation. There were no major clouds blocking the northern sky during the time I observed.

Sky conditions: Twilight was strong because it was late spring and the evening sky did not become very dark. The Moon was not visible during my observation, so the sky was darker than on 1 May, although twilight and light pollution still limited faint objects.

Equipment: Naked-eye observation.

Objects:
- Big Dipper / Ursa Major: I could see the Big Dipper high in the sky. It was the clearest constellation pattern during this observation.
- Cassiopeia: I could see Cassiopeia lower toward the north-east. It was less prominent than Ursa Major because it was lower and the northern sky was still bright.
- Orion: I could not see Orion.
- Sirius: I could not identify Sirius.
- Pleiades: I could not see the Pleiades.
- Milky Way: I could not see the Milky Way. Even without the bright Moon, the twilight and Stockholm light pollution made the sky too bright.
- Moon: I did not see the Moon during the evening observation.
- Venus: I could not confidently identify Venus because it was low in the bright western sky.
- Jupiter: I saw Jupiter after sunset. It was one of the easiest bright objects to identify, although it was lower as the evening continued.
- Mars: I could not see Mars.

Notes:
Compared with 1 May, the sky was darker because I did not see the Moon in the evening. However, the late spring twilight still made faint-object observing difficult. The most useful targets were the bright planets and the northern constellations, especially the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia.
